How to resize an image without losing quality

Written and tested by freeresizer · Updated 9 November 2026 · 8 minute read · How we test

“Don’t lose quality” is a reasonable goal, but it hides two very different jobs. Making a photo smaller is normal and almost always looks fine. Making a small photo bigger cannot add detail that was never captured. Once you know which one you are doing, every other decision — dimensions, aspect ratio, format — becomes straightforward.

The short version

Resize in pixels and keep Keep aspect ratio ticked. Never upscale by more than about 20–30% if the picture must look sharp. For a website, 1600 px on the long edge is plenty for most photos; for a profile picture, upload at least the size the platform displays.

Pixels are the only number that matters on screen

A digital image is a grid of pixels. A 3000 × 2000 photo is exactly that: 3000 pixels wide and 2000 tall, or six megapixels. On a screen, that is the whole story. Browsers and phones ignore the DPI value stored inside the file and simply map image pixels to screen pixels.

DPI (dots per inch) only becomes real when something physical is produced — a print, a poster, a scanned form. A 300 DPI A4 page needs about 2480 × 3508 pixels, no matter what number is written in the file’s metadata. This is why “set it to 300 DPI” is often unhelpful advice on its own: what the print shop actually needs is enough pixels for the printed size.

Pixels needed for common print sizes at 300 DPI
Print sizePixels at 300 DPI
Passport photo, 35 × 45 mm413 × 531
10 × 15 cm (4 × 6 in)1181 × 1772
A4, 21 × 29.7 cm2480 × 3508
A3, 29.7 × 42 cm3508 × 4961

Downscaling versus upscaling

Downscaling throws pixels away. Done from a good original, it usually looks better than the original at 100% zoom, because downsampling averages away noise. A 12 megapixel photo reduced to 1600 px for a blog post will look crisp.

Upscaling has to invent pixels. The resizer interpolates between existing ones, which softens edges and enlarges any existing blur. A 600 px logo stretched to 2000 px will be visibly soft, and no setting on this site — or any other ordinary resizer — can recover detail that was never recorded. If a client asks for a large version of a small file, the honest answer is that the source needs to be re-exported, not upscaled.

Rule of thumb: resizing down by any amount is safe. Resizing up by up to ~20% is usually invisible. Beyond 50% upscaling, expect softness; beyond 100%, expect mush.

Aspect ratio: the difference between resizing and stretching

Aspect ratio is width divided by height. A 3000 × 2000 photo is 3:2. If you type 800 × 800 and let the tool stretch it, the faces in the photo will be distorted. The resizer avoids that when Keep aspect ratio is ticked: you set one side and the other follows.

Sometimes the target is a fixed square — an Instagram post, a profile picture, a marketplace thumbnail. In that case the picture has to be cropped, not stretched. This site’s resizer fits the image inside the target rather than distorting it, so the result keeps its proportions; if a hard square is required, crop first in your phone’s photo editor, then resize.

Step by step with the Resize tool

  1. Choose Resize on the home page and add your image. It stays on your device.
  2. Pick a unit. Use Pixels when a form or platform gives exact dimensions. Use Percentage when you simply want the picture smaller or larger.
  3. Tick “Keep aspect ratio” unless you deliberately want a different shape. With it on, typing a width fills in the matching height.
  4. Set the output format. JPG for photos, PNG for logos and screenshots with text or transparency, WebP for websites. The quality slider matters for JPG and WebP only.
  5. Download and check the reported dimensions under the preview before you use the file.

If the goal is “small enough to upload” rather than an exact size, skip the pixel fields and use Resize to KB instead. It searches for the best trade-off between dimensions and compression for the limit you type.

Why a resized photo sometimes looks worse than expected

  • The source was already compressed. Repeatedly saving a JPEG at low quality stacks artefacts. Always start from the original.
  • The original was soft. Camera shake and missed focus cannot be fixed by resizing.
  • The screen is zoomed. A 1080 px image viewed at 200% on a high-density display will show its pixels; that is not a fault of the resize.
  • Text was in the image. Text is made of sharp edges, and JPEG compression treats edges harshly. Use PNG for screenshots and graphics with type.

Frequently asked questions

Can I increase resolution without blur?

Not with an ordinary resizer. Upscaling interpolates; it cannot recover detail. The only real fix is a higher-resolution original, a re-export from the design file, or a dedicated AI upscaler — and even those invent plausible detail rather than restoring the truth.

Does changing DPI change the file?

Changing only the DPI number changes metadata, not pixels. The image still contains the same width and height. For print, set the pixel dimensions instead.

Is it better to resize or to compress?

They solve different problems. Resizing changes the number of pixels; compression changes how those pixels are stored. For an upload limit, do both in the right order: resize to sensible dimensions, then compress to the size limit.

Will resizing remove the EXIF data?

Yes. Re-encoding an image through a canvas drops most metadata, including camera details and GPS location. That is usually good for privacy online; keep the original if you need the metadata.

How to convert images between JPG, PNG and WebP

Written and tested by freeresizer · Updated 9 November 2026 · 6 minute read · How we test

Most image problems blamed on “conversion” are really format problems. JPG has no transparency and re-compresses every time it is saved. PNG keeps everything but refuses to shrink. WebP is smaller than both, yet some upload forms still reject it. This guide explains what each format does before you press the button.

The short version

Use JPG for photographs and strict upload portals, PNG for screenshots, logos and anything with transparency, and WebP for a modern website you control. Converting changes how pixels are stored, not how many there are.

The three formats at a glance

What you are actually choosing between
FormatCompressionTransparencyTypical sizeBest for
JPGLossy — detail is discarded each saveNoSmallest for photosPhotographs, email, exam and job portals, anything that must open everywhere
PNGLossless — pixels are kept exactlyYesOften 2–5× a JPG for the same photoScreenshots, logos, line art, images with transparency
WebPLossy or lossless, your choiceYesUsually 25–35% smaller than JPGModern websites and apps, where you know the browser

What changes when you convert

Three things change, and one does not.

  • The pixel dimensions stay the same. A 1200 × 800 image remains 1200 × 800. Only the way those pixels are stored changes. If you need fewer pixels, use the Resize tool instead.
  • The pixels are re-encoded. Converting a JPG to JPG, or saving at a low quality, discards a little more detail every time. Convert from the original when you can.
  • Transparency survives only in PNG and WebP. Saving a transparent logo as JPG fills the empty area with white. If the background must stay clear, keep PNG or WebP.
  • Camera metadata is not copied. The output is drawn fresh from the pixels, so EXIF fields such as the capture date and GPS location do not travel with the new file. That is usually good for privacy and occasionally annoying when you wanted the date kept.

What the quality slider does. The slider only affects JPG and lossy WebP. PNG is lossless, so the slider is ignored and the file may even grow. Start at 85–90%: the difference from 100% is hard to see, and the file is much smaller.

Which output should you pick?

  • A photograph going to a form or a person: JPG at 85–90%. It is the format every portal accepts.
  • A screenshot with text: PNG. Text edges stay crisp; JPEG turns them fuzzy and adds coloured fringing.
  • A logo with a transparent background: PNG, or WebP if the destination is a website you control.
  • A website image: WebP when you can serve a fallback, JPG otherwise. Smaller files load faster and help page speed scores.
  • Something with an unknown destination: JPG. It is the safest default in 2026.

Step by step

  1. Open the Convert tool. On the home page it is the third card, marked with the loop icon. Conversion happens inside your browser; the image is not uploaded.
  2. Add your image. Drag it onto the drop area or press Choose image. JPG, PNG, WebP, GIF and BMP are accepted.
  3. Select the output format. Choose JPG, PNG or WebP under Output format. The preview updates immediately, including what happens to transparency.
  4. Set the quality if the format uses it. 85–90% is a good starting point for JPG and WebP. Leave it alone for PNG.
  5. Download. Check the file size shown under the preview, then press Download. Your original file is untouched.

When the converted file is bigger

This surprises people, but it is normal in three cases:

  • JPG to PNG. PNG keeps every pixel exactly, so a photo expands. Use PNG only when you need lossless detail or transparency.
  • Quality set to 100%. Maximum quality is rarely worth the size. 85–90% looks the same on a screen.
  • WebP that gets rejected. Some older apps, email clients and government portals only accept JPG or PNG, so you end up converting back. Check the destination's requirements before choosing WebP.

Formats this tool cannot open

HEIC and HEIF files from recent iPhones are not accepted, because browsers do not decode them consistently. If iPhone Photos gave you a HEIC file, export it as JPG first: open the photo, tap Share, then Copy Photo or Save to Files after changing the format in Settings. RAW files from cameras have the same problem; export a JPG from your camera software first.

Animated GIFs are accepted, but the conversion treats them as a still image and keeps the first frame. Animated WebP output is also not supported — this tool converts single images.

Frequently asked questions

Does converting an image reduce its quality?

PNG to PNG: no. JPG to JPG, or JPG to WebP: slightly, because both are lossy. PNG to JPG loses transparency and some detail, and converting back to PNG cannot bring that detail back.

Why did my transparent logo get a white background?

You saved as JPG, which has no transparency. Choose PNG or WebP instead. A white box, or a black one, is exactly what will print or upload.

Can I convert a PDF page to an image?

Yes, but not with the Convert tool. Use PDF to JPG, which renders each page as an image at a resolution you choose.

Can I convert several images at once?

The Convert tool works on one image at a time. If a whole folder needs the same format, repeat the four clicks per file — the drop area resets as soon as you add the next image.

Does converting remove my location data?

Yes. The new file is created from the pixels only, so EXIF metadata such as GPS coordinates and camera model is not carried over.

Is the image uploaded anywhere?

No. The conversion happens in your browser and the file never leaves your device. See the Privacy Policy for details.

How to pick colors from an image and copy the HEX code

Written and tested by freeresizer · Updated 9 November 2026 · 5 minute read · How we test

A client sends a photo and asks for “that green”. A logo needs its exact blue in a stylesheet. A theme has to start from a picture rather than a brand sheet. A color picker turns any pixel into values you can paste into code, a design tool or a message.

The short version

Open Color Picker, add the image, then click the spot you want. The colour is shown as HEX, RGB and HSL; every value is a button that copies it. The palette below the canvas lists up to eight dominant colours, each clickable.

What the tool shows you

  • A live colour tile. Move the pointer over a desktop image to preview the HEX value under the cursor; click to lock that pixel into the tile.
  • Three notations. The same colour appears as HEX, RGB and HSL. Click a button to copy that exact string.
  • A palette of main colours. The tool groups similar pixels and returns up to eight colours that are clearly different from each other, skipping translucent areas. Click a swatch to copy its HEX value.
  • Full-resolution sampling. The click reads the pixel at the image's real resolution, not the scaled preview, so a small button or a thin border can be picked accurately.

HEX, RGB and HSL in one table

The three ways the same colour is written
NotationExampleUsed for
HEX#FF0000CSS, design tools, brand guidelines, almost every colour field on the web
RGBrgb(255, 0, 0)CSS, canvas code, screen work where you want to adjust one channel
HSLhsl(0, 100%, 50%)Building a palette: keep the hue and change lightness for shades and tints

The teal used across this site is #0A7D78, which is rgb(10, 125, 120) and hsl(177, 85%, 26%). Three spellings, one colour. Which one you copy depends only on where it will be pasted.

Where these values get used

  • CSS custom properties. Store the colour once as --brand: #0A7D78; and reuse it, instead of retyping a hex code in twenty places.
  • Text on a coloured background. Pick the background from the image, then check whether black or white text is readable on it. The tool reports the colour; the contrast decision is still yours.
  • Matching a photograph. A banner built around an event photo looks intentional when its accent colour is sampled from the photo, not guessed.
  • Brand sheets. When a client supplies only a logo, sampling the flat areas gives you a defensible palette starting point.
  • Print and paint. Use the values as a reference, not as an exact recipe — see the accuracy note below.

Step by step

  1. Open the Color Picker. It is the fifth card on the home page, marked with the palette icon. Everything runs in your browser.
  2. Add an image. Drag it onto the drop area or press Choose image. JPG, PNG, WebP, GIF and BMP all work.

If copying does nothing, your browser may be blocking clipboard access. Select the value shown on the button and copy it manually with the keyboard. This is a browser permission, not an upload.

Getting a usable colour from a photo

A photo is not a colour chart. The same wall reads warm in evening light and cool in shade, and JPEG compression adds tiny variations even inside a flat area. Three habits keep the result predictable:

  • Sample the middle of a flat area, not an edge where the colour blends with its neighbour.
  • Expect slight differences between clicks. Two clicks a few pixels apart can return #2F6F6C and #306E6D. Both are correct samples; pick the one that matches the overall impression.
  • Prefer the palette when you need a theme. The eight dominant colours are averaged across many pixels, so they are more stable than a single sample.

Accuracy limits worth knowing

The tool reports the pixel values stored in the file. That is exactly what a browser will display, which is what matters for CSS. It is not a measurement of the object in the real world: lighting, white balance and the camera's colour processing all change what was recorded. For print, convert the reference to CMYK in a design application and proof it — a bright screen colour can land outside what a printer can reproduce.

How to turn photos into a PDF that prints correctly

Written and tested by freeresizer · Updated 9 November 2026 · 8 minute read · How we test

Any tool can wrap a JPEG in a PDF. Whether the result looks like a scanned document depends on four settings: page size, orientation, margin and resolution — and on how the photo was taken in the first place. This guide covers all five, in the order that matters.

The short version

Use A4 unless you are in the US or Canada (US Letter), set orientation to Automatic, keep a 10 mm margin, and leave quality at 90%. Put the pages in order before you create the file, because page order follows the list exactly.

When a PDF is the right format

PDFs keep a fixed page layout, so the recipient sees the same thing you do. That is why they are required for admissions, visa and passport applications, rent agreements, invoices attached to email, insurance claims and any form where page order matters. A folder of JPEGs attached to an email rarely survives that trip intact — phone mail apps reorder them, compress them differently, or strip them entirely.

Choosing a fixed page size fits the photo inside the printable area and centres it, keeping its proportions. Nothing is cropped and nothing is stretched. If a photo has a very different shape from the page, you will see white space above and below — that is correct behaviour, not a bug. Forcing the photo to fill the page would distort it.

How many pixels do you need?

Print resolution is usually quoted as DPI. For a readable document, 150 DPI is the practical minimum; 300 DPI is the standard for anything that will be printed sharply. Because an A4 page is 210 × 297 mm, that works out to:

The Photo to PDF tool caps each image at 3000 px on the long edge before placing it, which keeps the PDF a sensible size while staying close to 300 DPI for A4. What actually goes wrong is focus, lighting and angle.

Step by step

  1. Prepare the photos first. If a page is a photo of paper, run it through the scanner workflow so the background is white and the text is straight. A grey, shadowed photo stays grey and shadowed inside the PDF.
  2. Open Photo to PDF on the home page and add every image. Multi-select works; you can also drag a group of files.
  3. Put the pages in order using the arrows in the file list. Page 1 of the PDF is the first file in the list.
  4. Choose page size and orientation. A4 and Automatic cover most cases. Set a margin of 10 mm unless the form asks for a full-bleed page, in which case use 0.
  5. Set quality. 90% is a good default. Drop to 70–80% only if the PDF must fit an email limit; below that, small text starts to smear.
  6. Press Create PDF and open the file to check page order and orientation before sending it anywhere.

File size versus clarity

Each page is stored as a JPEG, so the quality slider is doing the real work. A ten-page document of clean, high-contrast scans at 90% is typically a few megabytes. Two things blow the size up: full-colour photos with lots of texture, and very high-resolution images that were not needed. If an upload limit is tight, convert the pages to black and white first — flat white backgrounds compress dramatically better than photographic ones.

Privacy note: the PDF is assembled inside your browser. Nothing is uploaded, which matters for ID documents, contracts and medical paperwork. Close the tab when you are finished.

Common mistakes

  • Sending the wrong order. Reorder before creating the PDF; renaming afterwards means redoing the whole file.
  • Using “Same as image” for a printed form. The printer then scales each page differently, and margins jump around.
  • Photographing at an angle. The page becomes a trapezoid, and no page-size setting fixes perspective. Retake it straight above the paper.
  • Leaving shadows across the page. Move away from the light source or use the scanner’s brightness control.
  • Sending a 40 MB file to an email address with a 10 MB limit. Lower the quality or split the PDF.

How to convert PDF pages to JPG or PNG images

Written and tested by freeresizer · Updated 9 November 2026 · 6 minute read · How we test

A PDF keeps a layout fixed, which is exactly what you want for sending a document and exactly what you do not want when you need one page inside a slide, a chat message or a web page. Converting pages to images solves that — if you choose the right resolution before you start.

The short version

Add the PDF, pick 1.5x for screens or 2x for printing, leave JPG at 90%, keep the Pages box empty to convert everything, then press Convert. One page downloads directly; several pages download as a ZIP.

What conversion actually does

The tool opens the PDF in your browser, draws each page onto a canvas at the resolution you choose, and saves that canvas as an image. The page becomes pixels: the layout, fonts and images look the same, but the text is no longer selectable or searchable. That is the trade you are making, and for a slide, a thumbnail or a chat attachment it is the right one.

Choose the resolution first

Resolution decides how much detail each page carries and, with it, the file size. The letter after the number is a multiplier of a screen-quality render; the approximate print equivalent is in the second column.

What each setting is for
SettingApproximate resolutionUse it for
Standard (1x)≈ 96 DPIThumbnails, quick previews, page images for a phone screen
Good (1.5x)≈ 144 DPISlides, email, web pages, most screen use — the best all-round choice
High (2x)≈ 192 DPIPrinting an A4 page, zooming in to read small text
Very high (3x)≈ 288 DPILarge prints, cropping into a detailed diagram, archival copies

Higher is not automatically better. A 3x render of a forty-page report can be hundreds of megabytes, and a scanned page will not gain real detail just because the output is larger.

JPG or PNG

  • JPG is the right default for scanned pages, photographs and anything with continuous tone. The quality slider applies: 90% is visually clean, and 70–80% is fine when the file must stay small.
  • PNG is better for slides, charts, line drawings and pages with sharp text. It is lossless, so the quality slider is ignored and files are larger than JPG.

Coloured backgrounds and thin lines survive PNG much better. Photographs and textured paper survive JPG much better. When in doubt, convert one page of each and compare at 100%.

Converting only some pages

The Pages box accepts a simple list. Leave it blank for the whole document, or type a combination:

  • 3 — just page 3
  • 2-5 — pages 2, 3, 4 and 5
  • 1,4,7-9 — page 1, page 4 and pages 7 to 9

Spaces around the commas are ignored, duplicates are removed and page numbers outside the document are skipped. If nothing valid remains, the tool tells you how many pages the PDF actually has.

Step by step

  1. Open PDF to JPG. It is the last card on the home page, marked with the book icon. The PDF is read on your device.
  2. Add the PDF. Drag it onto the drop area or press Choose file. Only PDF files are accepted here.
  3. Pick a resolution. Start with 1.5x for screen use or 2x if the images will be printed.
  4. Choose the format. JPG for scans and photos, PNG for slides and text-heavy pages. Set JPG quality to 90% unless the file must be small.
  5. Limit the pages if needed. Type a range such as 2-5. Leave the box empty to convert every page.
  6. Press Convert. Each page appears as a preview with its file size. Download a single page from its link, or press Download all (ZIP) for a folder of images.

Privacy and the internet. The PDF itself never leaves your device. The PDF reader component is downloaded once from a public library CDN, so the first conversion needs an internet connection; after that the browser may reuse the cached copy. For confidential documents, close the tab when you are done and delete the downloaded images if they are only needed temporarily.

Scanned PDFs versus text PDFs

If the PDF was made by a scanner, every page already is an image wrapped inside the PDF. Converting it again at 1.5x or 2x copies that image faithfully; converting at 3x only makes a larger copy of the same detail. If the PDF was generated from a word processor, the page is real text and a 2x render gives you sharp, readable text — but the moment it becomes an image, you can no longer select or search it. Keep the original PDF as the master copy.

When conversion fails

  • Password-protected PDF. The reader cannot open an encrypted document. Remove the password in a PDF application first, then convert the unlocked copy.
  • Damaged file. A truncated download often fails partway through. Download the PDF again from the source.
  • No internet on the first run. The reader library cannot load. Reconnect and try again.
  • A very large document. Conversion is done page by page in the browser; a long, high-resolution PDF simply takes longer. Convert only the pages you need.

Safe zones: the part everyone forgets

  • Text and fine patterns. Sharp edges are exactly what JPEG compression damages. Heavy grain, tiny type and dense patterns turn muddy first.

How to watermark images without ruining them

Written and tested by freeresizer · Updated 9 November 2026 · 7 minute read · How we test

A watermark has one job: make it obvious where an image came from, without becoming the most distracting thing in it. That is a question of size, opacity and placement — three settings people usually set too aggressively. This guide covers the choices, then shows a repeatable batch workflow.

The short version

Text or logo at roughly 8–15% of the image width, opacity 40–60%, placed in a corner. Use a centred or tiled mark only when you genuinely need to stop reuse. Export as JPG for photos, PNG for graphics.

What a watermark can and cannot do

A watermark is a credit line and a deterrent, not a security measure. Anyone determined enough can crop around a corner mark or clone it out. What a watermark does well is stop casual copying — a screenshot reposted to another account still carries your name — and make it easy for someone to find the original. Treat it as signage, not a lock.

For work you sell, the stronger approach is to publish a reduced-size preview with a visible mark and deliver the full-resolution file only to the client, without a watermark unless they ask for one.

Size: percentage of width, not pixels

The same watermark must work on a 1080 px Instagram image and a 6000 px camera file. That is why the size control is a percentage of image width rather than a pixel value. As a starting point:

  • 8–10% for a discreet credit in a corner.
  • 12–15% for a centred mark or a logo that must stay legible on a phone.
  • Above 20% only for deliberate, all-over tiling.

For text, 10% of a 1080 px image means roughly 108 px tall glyphs — comfortably readable in a feed. The same setting on a 4000 px file gives a 400 px mark, which looks identical when the image is displayed at the same physical size.

Opacity, contrast and the “invisible after compression” problem

Platforms re-encode images, and faint grey text on a mid-tone background is exactly what JPEG compression erases. Two fixes:

  • Use contrast, not opacity, for subtlety. White text with a thin dark outline, or a dark mark with a light outline, reads clearly at 40% opacity where plain grey text would vanish.
  • Avoid busy areas. A watermark over foliage or fabric is much harder to read than the same mark over sky or a wall. Move it to the calmest corner.

How to scan a document with your phone

Written and tested by freeresizer · Updated 9 November 2026 · 7 minute read · How we test

A phone photo of a form is not a scan. It is a photograph of paper on a table, with a grey cast, a shadow from your hand and a slight tilt. All three can be fixed in about a minute — most of it before you press the shutter.

The short version

Put the page on a dark, flat surface near a window, shoot straight down from directly above, and fill the frame. Then open the Image Scanner, choose Black & white for text or Colour, auto-enhanced for stamps and signatures, and export.

Step 1: shoot it properly

No amount of contrast adjustment can rescue a blurry or skewed photo. Five habits do most of the work:

  • Flat page, hard surface. A bed or a lap bends the paper, and the text distorts along the curve.
  • Dark background. A dark table or a sheet of black paper gives the page a clean edge to detect visually, and stops stray marks from becoming “content”.
  • Diffuse daylight, no direct sun. Stand near a window, out of the direct beam. Direct light creates glare on glossy paper.
  • No flash. The flash makes a bright hotspot in the middle of the page and darkens the corners.
  • Shoot straight above the centre. Stand so the camera is parallel to the page. Shooting from an angle turns a rectangle into a trapezoid, and this tool rotates but does not correct perspective.

Fill the frame with the page, but leave a small margin — you need a little background to see the edges when cropping later.

Step 2: choose a scan mode

The four modes and when each one wins
ModeWhat it doesUse it for
Colour, auto-enhancedKeeps colour, lifts contrast, whitens the paperStamps, signatures in blue ink, ID cards, anything where colour carries meaning
Black & white documentConverts to two tones using a sensitivity thresholdPrinted text, forms, printed receipts — the smallest, sharpest result
GrayscaleRemoves colour but keeps shadingPencil notes, photographs of pages, aged paper with stains
Original colourNo enhancementDocuments where an accurate colour record matters

Black and white is the best default for plain text: the file is small, the text is crisp, and faint scanner noise disappears. Switch to colour whenever a stamp, seal or handwritten signature must stay recognisable.

Step 3: tune the three sliders

  • Brightness lifts or darkens the whole page. If the paper looks grey, raise it slightly; if thin print is disappearing, lower it.
  • Contrast separates ink from paper. A little extra contrast fixes a dull phone photo; too much turns thin strokes into blobs.
  • Black & white sensitivity sets the cut-off between paper and ink. Raise it if light pencil or faint print is being erased; lower it if the page is filling with black speckles or shadow patches.

Work in this order: brightness until the page is light, contrast until the text is solid, sensitivity last to clean up the background.

Step 4: straighten and crop

The scanner rotates in 90° steps with the left and right buttons. If the page is tilted by five degrees, the honest fix is another photo with the phone held straight — the rotation buttons are for landscape pages and upside-down shots, not for crooked framing.

After rotating, glance at the whole preview: all four edges of the page should be inside the frame, with no fingers or table edge showing.

Step 5: export

  • Download image saves a JPG, PNG or WebP, depending on the format you pick. Use JPG for documents and PNG if the page contains fine line art or text that must stay razor sharp.
  • Save as PDF places the scan on an A4 page with an 8 mm margin, which is what most offices and portals expect.
  • For a multi-page document, scan each page, then combine them with the Photo to PDF tool in page order.
